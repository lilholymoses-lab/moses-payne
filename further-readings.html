Further Readings

Sources, references, and further exploration

Purpose of these notes. These notes are a source map for the project. They identify places where the main text makes historical, doctrinal, mathematical, scientific, or philosophical claims that should carry formal endnote citations in a publication-ready edition. They also give readers reliable routes for further study. They do not convert the project’s analogies into proofs, and they do not replace specialist review.

1. Scope and method

The author’s governing claim is presented here as a metaphysical and theological proposal, not as an empirical scientific theory. For orientation on theology-and-science method, see Ian G. Barbour, When Science Meets Religion (HarperOne, 2000); John F. Haught, Deeper Than Darwin: The Prospect for Religion in the Age of Evolution (Westview Press, 2003); and Alister E. McGrath, Scientific Theology, 3 vols. (T&T Clark, 2001–2003).

2. Trinity and creeds

See the Nicene Creed (325) and the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed (381), conveniently collected in Norman P. Tanner, ed., Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils, vol. 1 (Georgetown University Press, 1990). For modern accounts of pro-Nicene theology, see Lewis Ayres, Nicaea and Its Legacy: An Approach to Fourth-Century Trinitarian Theology (Oxford University Press, 2004); and Khaled Anatolios, Retrieving Nicaea: The Development and Meaning of Trinitarian Doctrine (Baker Academic, 2011).

3. Perichoresis

For classical Trinitarian discussion, see Gregory of Nazianzus, Theological Orations 27–31; Gregory of Nyssa, On Not Three Gods; Augustine, The Trinity (De Trinitate); and John of Damascus, An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith, book I. Perichoresis is especially associated with later Greek theological vocabulary and should not be used as if it were a simple technical synonym for any created relation.

4. Kenosis and Christology

Primary texts include Philippians 2:5–11, John 1:1–18, Colossians 1:15–20, and Hebrews 1:1–4. For conciliar Christology, see the Definition of Chalcedon (451) in Tanner, Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils. For constructive modern Christology, see Sarah Coakley, God, Sexuality, and the Self (Cambridge University Press, 2013); and Kathryn Tanner, Jesus, Humanity and the Trinity: A Brief Systematic Theology (Fortress Press, 2001).

5. Torus and topology

For the relevant mathematics, see James R. Munkres, Topology, 2nd ed. (Prentice Hall, 2000); and Allen Hatcher, Algebraic Topology (Cambridge University Press, 2002). A torus is homeomorphic to (S¹ × S¹); it is a compact, connected, orientable surface without boundary, with two independent basic one-dimensional cycle classes.

The Clifford torus is a particular torus embedded in the three-dimensional sphere (S³), which lies in four-dimensional Euclidean space. Although both an ordinary torus and a Clifford torus share the product structure (S¹ × S¹), their embeddings differ. The Clifford torus provides a way to examine how two independent circular dimensions belong to a single geometric structure within a higher-dimensional setting.

These mathematical properties support the geometric description only. The project’s use of the torus and Clifford torus as images of unity-in-distinction, relational organization, and becoming is philosophical and analogical, not a mathematical theorem. The metaphysical meanings assigned to coordinates in the project are interpretations developed within the framework, not meanings established by the mathematics itself.

6. Fibonacci and the golden ratio

For a mathematically careful introduction, see Thomas Koshy, Fibonacci and Lucas Numbers with Applications, 2nd ed. (Wiley, 2018). The Fibonacci recurrence provides a simple example of a rule in which each term depends on preceding terms. Ratios of successive Fibonacci numbers approach the golden ratio, and Fibonacci numbers can be used to construct square arrangements whose proportions approximate a golden spiral.

The mathematical properties of the sequence and golden ratio should be distinguished from their philosophical interpretation in this project. The phi spiral is used to represent the unfolding of distinction and the development of ordered form through relationship. This is a conceptual correspondence, not evidence that biological forms, consciousness, or cosmic history universally follow a Fibonacci law.

7. Arthur M. Young

For Young’s own framework, consult Arthur M. Young, The Reflexive Universe: Evolution of Consciousness (Delacorte Press, 1976), and The Geometry of Meaning (Delacorte Press, 1976). The comparison in this project is the author’s; readers should consult Young directly rather than assume that the present layers, Trinitarian interpretation, or account of kenosis reproduce his system.

8. Quantum fields and particles

For standard introductions to quantum field theory, see Michael E. Peskin and Daniel V. Schroeder, An Introduction to Quantum Field Theory (Westview Press, 1995); and Mark Srednicki, Quantum Field Theory (Cambridge University Press, 2007). In quantum field theory, particles are commonly modeled as excitations of quantum fields, but the ontology of particles, fields, and states remains philosophically contested. See David Wallace, The Emergent Multiverse (Oxford University Press, 2012), especially discussions of quantum theory and ontology.

The project’s Field → Particle crossing uses this scientific context to ask whether the transition from an organized field description to a particle involves a distinction between structure and actuality. The philosophical identity test developed in Project Two is the author’s framework for investigating that question; it should not be confused with an established result of quantum field theory.

9. Autocatalysis and origin of life

For autocatalytic sets and formal models of chemical closure, see Stuart A. Kauffman, The Origins of Order (Oxford University Press, 1993); Wim Hordijk and Mike Steel, “Autocatalytic Sets and the Origin of Life,” Entropy 12 (2010): 1733–1742; and Peter Schuster, “What Is Life? A Systems Dynamics View,” in The Molecular Origins of Life, ed. André Brack (Cambridge University Press, 1998).

Autocatalysis is relevant to origin-of-life research, but an autocatalytic network is not thereby identical with a living organism. Project Two uses the transition from chemistry to autocatalytic organization to investigate whether a self-producing network represents a genuinely new form of organization rather than merely a greater quantity of chemical activity.

10. Self-maintenance and autopoiesis

For organizational closure and autopoiesis, see Humberto R. Maturana and Francisco J. Varela, Autopoiesis and Cognition: The Realization of the Living (D. Reidel, 1980); and Evan Thompson, Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind (Harvard University Press, 2007). For systems approaches in biology, see Denis Noble, The Music of Life: Biology Beyond Genes (Oxford University Press, 2006).

For the classical physiological account of homeostasis, see Walter B. Cannon, The Wisdom of the Body (W. W. Norton, 1932). Cannon’s work provides historical context for the regulation of physiological conditions, while autopoiesis and organizational closure address broader questions about the organization and maintenance of living systems.

These traditions are debated and should be presented as influential accounts of living organization, not as a final consensus definition of life. Their relevance to Project Two lies in the distinction between a system that maintains particular conditions and an organization that actively renews the relationships through which it continues to exist.

11. Consciousness

There is no single accepted scientific theory of consciousness. For the Global Neuronal Workspace account, see Stanislas Dehaene, Consciousness and the Brain (Viking, 2014); and Stanislas Dehaene, Jean-Pierre Changeux, and Lionel Naccache, “The Global Neuronal Workspace Model of Conscious Access,” in The Cambridge Handbook of Consciousness, ed. Philip David Zelazo, Morris Moscovitch, and Evan Thompson (Cambridge University Press, 2007). For contrasting approaches, see Masafumi Oizumi, Larissa Albantakis, and Giulio Tononi, “From the Phenomenology to the Mechanisms of Consciousness: Integrated Information Theory 3.0,” PLoS Computational Biology 10, no. 5 (2014): e1003588; and Victor A. F. Lamme, “Towards a True Neural Stance on Consciousness,” Trends in Cognitive Sciences 10, no. 11 (2006): 494–501.

These approaches offer different accounts of conscious access, information integration, and neural processing. They do not settle the metaphysical question of consciousness or establish that awareness is fundamental to reality.

12. Agency and free will

For philosophy of action and agency, see Alfred R. Mele, Effective Intentions: The Power of Conscious Will (Oxford University Press, 2009); and John Martin Fischer, My Way: Essays on Moral Responsibility (Oxford University Press, 2006). For relevant cognitive science and neuroscience, see Patrick Haggard, “Human Volition: Towards a Neuroscience of Will,” Nature Reviews Neuroscience 9 (2008): 934–46.

These sources do not settle metaphysical free will; they help distinguish action control, intention, deliberation, responsibility, and competing concepts of agency. Project Two’s Consciousness → Agency crossing uses these distinctions to ask whether conscious processing alone is sufficient to explain agency, or whether a further organization of relationships is required for a represented course of action to influence what a system attempts.

13. Scripture and theological interpretation

Biblical allusions in this project should be cited directly in the final edition. Key passages include Genesis 1–3; Matthew 28:19; John 1:1–18 and 17; Philippians 2:5–11; Colossians 1:15–20; and Hebrews 1:1–4. Interpretive claims about Adam, the Fall, Incarnation, Cross, reconciliation, and communion should be identified as theological readings within a Christian tradition rather than as conclusions of scientific inquiry.

14. Process philosophy

For a major philosophical account of reality as process, see Alfred North Whitehead, Process and Reality: An Essay in Cosmology, corrected ed., edited by David Ray Griffin and Donald W. Sherburne (Free Press, 1978). Whitehead’s process philosophy treats becoming, relations, and events as fundamental to understanding reality. It provides an important comparison for this project’s emphasis on becoming and generative relationship, although Whitehead’s metaphysical categories and account of God differ from the present framework. The comparison should clarify both affinities and differences rather than imply that the project reproduces Whitehead’s system.

15. Fundamental awareness

For a proposed framework integrating science, philosophy, and metaphysics around fundamental awareness, see Neil D. Theise and Menas C. Kafatos, “Fundamental Awareness: A Framework for Integrating Science, Philosophy and Metaphysics,” Communicative & Integrative Biology 9, no. 3 (2016): e1155010, https://doi.org/10.1080/19420889.2016.1155010.

Theise and Kafatos explore an account in which awareness is foundational to existence rather than merely a late product of material complexity. Their proposal is relevant to this project’s investigation of the source of reality and the emergence of consciousness, but its premises and scientific implications should be assessed independently rather than treated as established scientific consensus.

16. Similarity Theory

For a contemporary cosmological and philosophical framework connecting consciousness, time, dimensional structure, and recurring patterns across scales, see Simon Raphael, Similarity Theory, available at https://www.similaritytheory.com/.

The framework offers a comparative case for examining how consciousness, relational structure, and the organization of reality may be brought together in a unified account. Its proposed relationships among consciousness, time, and dimensions may be compared with this project’s account of Undivided Potential, relationship, and becoming. Similarities should be evaluated at the level of specific arguments and conceptual structures, without assuming that the two frameworks share the same starting principles or conclusions. Similarity Theory should be approached as a philosophical and cosmological proposal, not as an established scientific theory.

17. Emergence and levels of organization

For philosophical analysis of emergence and the distinction between a system’s underlying components and the novel forms that may arise through their organization, see Paul Humphreys, Emergence: A Philosophical Account (Oxford University Press, 2016). For a collection of influential philosophical and scientific approaches, see Mark A. Bedau and Paul Humphreys, eds., Emergence: Contemporary Readings in Philosophy and Science (MIT Press, 2008).

These works examine competing accounts of emergence, reduction, self-organization, and the appearance of new properties at different levels of organization. They provide a framework for evaluating the distinction developed in Project Two between an existing condition and a genuinely new form. They do not establish that the four crossings proposed in this project necessarily constitute instances of ontological emergence.

18. Potentiality and actuality

For a foundational philosophical account of potentiality and actuality, see Aristotle, Metaphysics, book IX (Theta). Aristotle examines the distinction between what something can be and its actuality, providing an important historical point of comparison for this project’s investigation of potential, becoming, and physical expression.

The comparison should preserve the differences between Aristotle’s account and the present framework’s concepts of Undivided Potential, relationship, and the generative process through which actuality emerges. Aristotle’s philosophical categories should not be treated as equivalent to the scientific distinctions examined in Project Two.
