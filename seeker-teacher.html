The Seeker and the Teacher

A fictional dialogue approaching the framework from another direction

The Book of the Undivided — a work of fiction about a seeker and a teacher. The teacher walks the seeker through the metaphysical framework of becoming, one idea at a time. The conversation never happened; the ideas in it are real.

Chapter I — The Man Who Asked Too Many Questions

There once lived a man who desired to understand the world. He was an ordinary man who worked, ate, slept, and occasionally wondered why he had to work so much just to afford the food he ate.

He had questions about the heavens, the earth, the nature of life, and the reason that anything existed at all. Unfortunately for those around him, he was not very good at keeping these questions to himself.

If a farmer planted a seed, the man asked how the seed became a tree. If a mason built a wall, he asked how the stones remained standing. And if someone told him that everything happened for a reason, he asked what the reason was and whether that reason itself had a reason. After some time, his neighbors learned to recognize the look upon his face that preceded a difficult question. Some began leaving whenever they saw it.

One afternoon, the man watched a farmer planting seeds in the soil.

He approached the farmer and asked, “How does the seed know to become a tree?”

The farmer wiped his brow. “I don’t know. I plant it, water it, and wait. If you want a tree by tomorrow, I suggest you speak to someone more powerful than me.”

The man was dissatisfied with this answer, though the farmer seemed satisfied enough to return to his work.

That evening, the man heard of a teacher who lived beneath an old tree beyond the village. They said the teacher possessed great wisdom, though no one could agree on whether he was the wisest man alive or simply the man least bothered by other people’s opinions. The man set out the following morning.

He found the teacher sitting beneath the tree, eating a piece of bread and watching a bird attempt to steal another piece from his hand.

“Are you the teacher?” the man asked.

“That depends,” the teacher replied, pulling the bread away from the bird. “Are you here to learn something, or have you come to tell me everything I am doing wrong?”

“I have questions about the nature of reality.”

The teacher sighed. “Reality. Of course. I was hoping for something simple, like the weather.” The man sat before him.

“I want to understand how everything came to be. I want to know what lies beneath the things we see.”

“A worthy pursuit,” said the teacher. “Though I should warn you that the deeper you look, the more questions you will find. It is a terrible arrangement. You solve one mystery and discover that it has several relatives.”

The man almost smiled, but his curiosity was too great to be distracted for long. The teacher studied him for a moment, then reached into a small pouch and placed a seed in the man’s palm.

“Tell me,” he said, “what do you see?”

“A seed.”

“Good. We shall make a philosopher of you yet.”

The man looked down at the tiny object resting in his hand.

The teacher leaned back against the tree.

“Now let us see whether you can tell me what a seed has to do with the beginning of the world.”

Chapter II — The Seed and the Tree

The teacher pointed toward the great tree spreading its branches above them.

“What may become of that seed?” he asked.

“A tree,” the man replied.

“And if I plant it in the middle of a stone floor and refuse to give it water?”

“It will not grow.”

“Then perhaps the seed requires more than a strong desire to become a tree.”

The man laughed. “It requires the right conditions.”

“Just so. The seed, the soil, the water, the light, and the conditions surrounding them all matter. You cannot understand the tree by looking only at the seed, as though the rest of the world were merely decoration.”

The teacher pointed toward the tree above them.

“Look at this old fellow. He began as something small, and now he gives shade to men who ask difficult questions.”

The man laughed, and the teacher continued.

“You see, the seed is not yet the tree. What is present and what may become are related, but they are not identical.”

“Then there is a difference between potential and actuality,” said the man.

“Yes. And I am pleased that you have discovered this before lunchtime. We may yet finish a lesson today.”

The man turned the seed between his fingers.

“But the seed does not become a tree by itself. It depends on the soil, the water, the sunlight, and everything else that allows it to grow.”

“Indeed.”

“And those things depend upon other things. The soil belongs to the earth. The sunlight comes from the heavens. Even the farmer depends upon the land to feed him.”

The teacher nodded, waiting for him to continue.

The man looked at the seed again.

“If everything depends upon something beyond itself, then what does the whole world depend upon?”

For the first time since their conversation began, the teacher stopped smiling.

He looked at the man as though he recognized a familiar question.

“There it is,” he said. “The little seed has become a very large problem.”

The man waited for an answer.

Instead, the teacher rose, brushed the dirt from his robe, and picked up his staff. “Come back tomorrow,” he said.

“Why tomorrow?”

“Because today you have discovered the question. Tomorrow, we shall see whether you can learn how to approach it.”

The man frowned.

“You could simply tell me what you know.”

“I could also carry you up the mountain, but then you would learn nothing about walking.” The teacher turned toward the path.

“Besides, I have eaten my bread, answered your questions, and lost half my breakfast to a bird. I have done enough for one morning.”

The man slipped the seed into his pocket and began the walk home.

That evening, he thought about the difference between the seed and the tree. The tree was not present in the seed in the same form that it would later possess, yet the seed was not unrelated to what it would become. Something about its present condition allowed a future form to emerge, given the right circumstances.

He began to wonder whether becoming was not simply a matter of one thing appearing after another. Perhaps it involved a deeper relationship between what was present, what was possible, and the conditions through which something new could take form.

But his larger question remained.

If every particular thing depended upon other things, could the beginning of reality itself be understood in the same way?

And if everything belonged to a greater whole, what did it mean for that whole to exist? The next morning, he returned to the teacher with these questions still in his mind.

Chapter III — The Circle in the Dust

The teacher was waiting beneath the old tree.

“You have returned,” he said.

“I have been thinking about the seed.”

“Then I hope you gave it a little rest. It has had a difficult life already.”

The man smiled and sat beside him.

“I have been thinking about something else, too. You said that the whole world might depend upon something. But if everything belongs to something greater, how can we ever arrive at the whole itself?”

The teacher picked up a fallen branch and drew a circle in the dirt.

“Tell me, before I drew this circle, was the earth here?”

“Yes.”

“And did the circle create the earth?”

“No. You just marked a part of it.”

“Excellent. We have established that I am not the creator of the earth. My neighbors will be relieved.”

The man smiled.

“Now look carefully,” the teacher continued. “The line allows you to distinguish what lies within the circle from what lies beyond it. Yet the earth has not been divided into two different substances. The line gives you a distinction without making the ground itself disappear.” The man traced the circle with his finger.

“So distinction does not necessarily mean separation?”

“Precisely. You are learning.”

“But the line still divides what is inside from what is outside.”

“It distinguishes them,” the teacher replied. “Whether that distinction must amount to complete separation is another question.”

He drew a second circle, this one inside the first.

“Imagine that you have been asked to understand the whole of reality. You begin by naming its parts: stars, planets, living creatures, and all the things you encounter. But naming the parts does not yet explain how they belong to the whole, nor how distinction first becomes possible.” The man looked at the two circles.

“Then what was there before the distinctions?” he asked.

“I call this the Undivided Potential. Do not imagine it as a very large object floating in an empty room. If the room and the object were already distinct, you would have begun too late.” The man frowned.

“You make it sound as though I have been asking the wrong question.”

“Not the wrong question. Only a question that carries an assumption you have not yet examined. It is like searching for the beginning of a road while refusing to ask what a road is.” “Then the Undivided is the Source from which all things may become?”

“That is how we shall begin to understand it. But remember: giving the mystery a name is not the same as solving it. Otherwise, I could explain the entire universe by writing Universe on a piece of paper and going home.”

The man laughed, but then grew thoughtful.

“If the Source is undivided, how can distinct things arise from it? How can the many emerge without the Whole ceasing to be one?”

The teacher placed the branch upon the ground.

“Now that,” he said, “is a question worth losing sleep over.”

“Will you answer it?”

“Tomorrow. Even the deepest mysteries must occasionally wait for breakfast.”

The man sighed.

“You have a remarkable talent for postponing answers.”

“And you have a remarkable talent for asking questions before you have finished considering the previous ones. Perhaps we are both being educated.”

The man returned home carrying the seed in his pocket and the question in his mind. For the first time, he began to suspect that the world was not merely a collection of things to be named, but a mystery of relationships to be understood.

Chapter IV — The Meadow of Many Trees

The following morning, the man rose earlier than he usually did.

The question had followed him through the night. He had turned it over in his mind while eating supper, while preparing for bed, and even after he had closed his eyes. By morning, he had reached no answer, though he had discovered several new ways to become confused.

He returned to the old teacher’s home just as the sun began to climb above the distant hills. The teacher was outside, tying a small bundle of bread into a cloth.

“You have come early,” the teacher observed.

“I could not stop thinking about what you said yesterday.”

“A dangerous habit. It often leads to more thinking.”

“I want to understand how the many can emerge from the One without the One ceasing to be one.”

The teacher tightened the knot around the bread.

“Then we should walk.”

“Walk?”

“Yes. I find that men sometimes understand more when they stop staring at me.”

The teacher took his staff, and the two set off along a narrow path beyond the village. They passed low stone walls, patches of wildflowers, and fields where the morning dew still clung to the grass. The air was cool, and the distant hills were softened by a thin veil of mist. Before long, the path opened into a broad meadow.

The man stopped.

Trees were scattered across the green expanse, each standing apart from the others. Some were tall and broad, their branches spreading like open arms. Others were young and slender, their leaves trembling in the breeze. One stood near a small stream, while another grew upon a gentle rise where the sunlight fell freely upon its crown.

They were not arranged in rows, nor did they appear to follow any plan that the man could see. Yet together they gave the meadow a beauty that none of them seemed to possess alone. The teacher watched him looking.

“Well?” he asked.

“It is beautiful.”

“A promising beginning. I feared you might ask me to explain the grass.”

The man smiled but continued studying the trees.

“They are all different,” he said. “Some are larger, some smaller. Some stand close together, while others are separated by long stretches of grass.”

“And yet?”

“And yet they belong to the same meadow.”

The teacher nodded.

“Tell me, does the meadow cease to be one meadow because it contains many trees?” “No.”

“Then must everything be identical in order to belong to one whole?”

The man looked across the field.

“No. I suppose not.”

“Good. For if everything had to be identical, this would be a rather disappointing meadow. One tree repeated a thousand times. No stream, no flowers, no hills in the distance. And certainly no old teacher to improve the scenery.”

“You seem very pleased with yourself this morning,” the man said.

“I slept well. It is a privilege you should try sometime.”

They continued walking through the grass.

After a while, the teacher stopped beside a young tree. Its trunk was thin, and its branches had only begun to spread.

“Look at this one,” he said. “And then look at the great tree near the stream. Are they the same?”

“No. They are different trees.”

“But do they share something?”

“They are both trees. They grow from the earth, receive the sunlight, and draw water through their roots.”

“And does the existence of one prevent the existence of the other?”

“No.”

“Then difference need not be an enemy of unity.”

The man considered this.

“Perhaps the unity of the meadow does not mean that the trees must lose their differences. Perhaps it means that their differences can belong together.”

The teacher’s expression softened.

“That is a better answer than you gave me yesterday.”

“I have had a whole night to improve.”

“And very little sleep, by the sound of it.”

The man laughed.

They walked farther until they reached the stream. Water moved quietly over the stones, reflecting fragments of sky between the shadows of the trees.

The man crouched and placed his hand in the water.

“The stream is different from the trees,” he said. “And the grass is different from the soil. Yet all of them belong to this place.”

“Indeed.”

“But I still do not understand how this helps us explain the beginning of reality. These things already exist. The trees have grown, the stream is flowing, and the meadow is here. We are looking at a world that has already become.”

The teacher leaned upon his staff.

“An excellent objection. Never become so fond of an analogy that you forget where it fails.” The man stood.

“So the meadow cannot show us how the first distinctions arose?”

“Not directly. It shows us something about how difference and unity can coexist. But it does not prove that reality began in the way we are imagining. We must be careful not to mistake an illustration for an explanation.”

The teacher picked up a small stone from the stream’s edge and turned it in his hand. “Still, the meadow gives us a question worth carrying farther. If many different things can belong to one whole, perhaps we should not imagine unity as though it were merely a thing among other things.”

“What do you mean?”

“Look around you. You can point to a tree, a flower, a stone, or a stream. Each has its own particular form. But where, precisely, is the meadow itself?”

The man looked about him.

“It is all around us.”

“Then show me where it ends.”

The man pointed toward the far edge of the grass, where the land rose into a low hill. “There, perhaps.”

“And if we walk beyond that hill?”

“Then we would find something else.”

“And if we walked beyond that, and beyond the next place, would we eventually find ourselves standing outside all of reality?”

The man opened his mouth, then closed it again.

The teacher smiled.

“There is no shame in silence. It is often the first sign that a question has finally caught up with you.”

The man looked toward the distant hills. He had spent much of his life thinking of the world as a collection of separate things: the house where he slept, the field where he worked, the river he crossed, and the sky above his head. Now he began to wonder whether the separation he perceived between them told only part of the story.

Perhaps the things he could name were distinctions within something greater.

“But if the Whole contains all things,” he said slowly, “then the Whole cannot be just another thing standing beside them.”

The teacher turned toward him.

“Now you are beginning to see the difficulty.”

“Then the Undivided Potential is not a tree, or a meadow, or even the sum of all the trees and meadows.”

“Not as we are presently using the term. The Undivided Potential names the Source before we speak of distinct things as though they were already separate and complete. We are trying to think about the condition from which distinction and becoming might be understood.” The man watched the stream moving around a stone.

“And yet,” he said, “if I call it a Source, I have already begun to describe it.” “Precisely.”

“Then every name I give it seems to bring me back to the very distinctions I am trying to understand.”

The teacher laughed quietly.

“You see why I eat breakfast before teaching? A man cannot live on questions alone.” He unwrapped the bread and broke it in two, offering half to his companion.

They sat beneath the shade of a broad tree, with the meadow stretching before them. For a time, neither spoke.

The man watched the branches moving gently overhead. Sunlight passed between the leaves, appearing and disappearing as the wind shifted them. The branches were distinct, yet they belonged to one tree. The tree stood apart from its neighbors, yet it belonged to the meadow. The meadow belonged to the land, and the land stretched beyond the limits of his sight. He began to sense that the question was not simply how one thing could produce another. It was how distinction could arise without requiring that everything be utterly separate from everything else.

At last, he turned to the teacher.

“Yesterday, you said that the Undivided Potential was the Source from which all things may become. But if nothing is yet distinguished, how can anything begin to take form?” The teacher brushed a crumb from his sleeve.

“Tomorrow,” he said, “we shall speak of possibility.”

The man sighed.

“Must every answer wait until tomorrow?”

“Not every answer. Only the important ones.”

“And what am I to do with this question until then?”

The teacher rose and offered him a hand.

“Carry it as you carried the seed. Do not demand that it become a tree before its time.” The man stood beside him, and together they began the walk back toward the village. Behind them, the trees remained scattered across the meadow, each distinct, each belonging to the same landscape.

And the man wondered whether the beginning of reality might be less like the making of separate things and more like the unfolding of distinctions within a unity that had never needed to be broken apart.

Chapter V — The Path of Possibility

The following morning, the man returned to the teacher beneath the old tree.

He had spent much of the night thinking about the meadow. He remembered the trees standing across the grass, each different, yet belonging to the same landscape. He remembered the question he had asked before they left.

If nothing was yet distinguished, how could anything begin to take form?

The teacher greeted him with a nod.

“You look troubled.”

“I am trying to understand how something can emerge from the Undivided without the Undivided ceasing to be one.”

“And have you succeeded?”

“No.”

“Good. At least we know you have not mistaken confusion for wisdom.”

The man sighed.

“Will you answer my question today?”

“I will try to help you see something. Whether that answers your question is for you to decide.” The teacher picked up his staff and began walking toward the meadow.

“Come. There is something I want you to look at.”

They followed the familiar path beyond the village. The morning sun had begun to warm the grass, and the dew shone upon the leaves of the trees. Before long, they reached the meadow they had visited the day before.

This time, however, the teacher did not stop to admire the trees.

He led the man toward the stream.

“You remember this place?” he asked.

“Of course. We sat beneath the tree over there.”

“And what did you notice about the stream yesterday?”

“That it belonged to the meadow, along with the trees, the grass, and the stones.” “An excellent answer. Though today I am less interested in where the stream belongs than in where it goes.”

The teacher pointed toward the water.

The stream flowed between the grassy banks, passing over smooth stones and around the roots of trees. In some places it moved swiftly, its surface breaking into little ripples. In others it widened and slowed, reflecting the branches overhead.

“Watch the water,” the teacher said.

The man stood beside him and watched.

After a while, the teacher picked up a small stone and placed it in the stream’s path. The water struck the stone and divided around it. One portion passed to the left, another to the right. A short distance downstream, the two currents came together again.

“What happened?” the teacher asked.

“The stone interrupted the water’s path.”

“Did it stop the stream?”

“No. The water moved around it.”

“Could the water have taken another path?”

“Yes. If the stone had been placed differently, or if the ground had been shaped another way, the water might have flowed differently.”

The teacher nodded.

“And yet the water did not take every path it could have taken. It took the paths available to it under the conditions before it.”

The man watched the currents reunite beyond the stone.

“So there were different possibilities, but only some became actual.”

“Precisely.”

The man considered this for a moment.

“Then possibility is not the same as actuality.”

“No more than the path the water might have taken is the same as the path it actually follows.” The teacher crouched and moved the stone slightly. The current changed again, sending a narrow ribbon of water toward the opposite bank.

“Then possibility depends upon conditions?”

“What becomes possible in a particular situation is shaped by the conditions that are present. Change the conditions, and different paths may open. Some may close.”

The man watched the stream with renewed interest.

“Then the world is full of things that could happen but do not.”

“Indeed. You could have remained in bed this morning.”

“I nearly did.”

“And yet here you are, standing beside a stream while an old man rearranges stones.” The man laughed.

“I begin to understand why your neighbors find you difficult.”

“My neighbors find me difficult because they have not yet learned to appreciate my methods.” “Or perhaps they have.”

The teacher looked at him.

“You are becoming dangerously comfortable speaking to me.”

They both laughed, and for a time they watched the water in silence.

Then the man spoke again.

“If possibility means what may become, where does possibility itself come from?”

The teacher did not answer immediately.

The man continued.

“The water can take different paths because the stream, the ground, and the stone already exist. But if we are speaking about the beginning of reality, we cannot simply assume that all

these conditions were already there. We are trying to understand how there could be anything to begin with.”

The teacher slowly nodded.

“You have noticed the difference between using an example to understand an idea and using it to explain the origin of everything. The stream helps us see how different possibilities can exist under different conditions. But it does not tell us, by itself, how possibility first arises.” The man considered this for a moment.

“Then why does possibility depend upon conditions? What determines those conditions before there are conditions?”

The teacher looked at him thoughtfully.

“Now you are asking about something deeper than the paths the water may take. You are asking what makes it possible for there to be conditions at all.”

The man nodded.

“Exactly. If the water requires the ground, the stone, and the force of the current in order to follow a particular path, then what accounts for the existence of those things? And if those conditions depend upon other conditions, where does the dependence begin?”

The teacher picked up a small stone and held it in his palm.

“Let us return to the question we began with. Remember the Undivided Potential?”

“The Source from which all things may become.”

“Just so, we must consider whether the conditions that allow things to take form ultimately arise from that Source. Before we speak of distinct things, their relationships, and the conditions governing their possibilities, we must ask what makes their emergence possible in the first place.”

The man studied the stone in the teacher’s hand.

“Then the Undivided Potential is not simply one condition among all the others?”

“No. If it were just one condition alongside the rest, we would have to ask what made that condition possible. We would have moved the question back another step without answering it. The Undivided Potential names the Source from which the distinctions, relationships, and conditions of becoming may arise. It is not another object within the world we are trying to explain, but what our inquiry seeks to understand as the ground of the world’s emergence.” The man looked toward the stream.

“Then the conditions do not explain themselves. Their existence must be understood in relation to the Source.”

“That is the direction of our inquiry,” the teacher replied. “But remember that we are developing an understanding, not pretending that a name alone has solved the mystery. We must still ask how what is undivided can give rise to distinction, how possibility takes form, and how relationships allow something new to become.”

The man looked disappointed.

“Then we have not answered the question.”

“Not completely.”

The teacher rested his staff against the bank and sat upon a flat stone.

“Tell me something. Before the water reached this stone, did the path to its left exist as the path the water actually took?”

“No. The water had not yet flowed around it.”

“But was that path possible?”

“Yes.”

“Then the actual path was not the only path that could have occurred. There was a range of possibilities, though only one particular movement unfolded under those conditions.” The man nodded slowly.

“And when the water moves, its path becomes part of what happens next.”

“Just so. The water that passes the stone continues downstream. It encounters new ground, new stones, and new turns. What has happened becomes part of the conditions for what happens afterward.”

The man looked farther along the stream, following its winding course through the meadow. “Then what becomes can help shape what becomes next.”

The teacher smiled.

“Now you are beginning to follow the stream instead of merely staring at it.”

The man watched the water disappear between the trees.

He had begun the morning thinking of possibility as little more than an unrealized future, something that waited to become actual. Now he was beginning to see that possibilities were

connected to conditions, and that what became actual could shape the possibilities that followed.

The world seemed less like a collection of finished objects and more like an unfolding movement.

Yet the greatest question remained.

“If possibility is the range of what can become,” he said, “and actuality is what does become, then what allows a possibility to take one form rather than another?”

The teacher rose and retrieved his staff.

“That is the question we must carry with us.”

“Where shall we go tomorrow?”

The teacher glanced toward the meadow, where the stream continued along its winding course. “Tomorrow, we shall look at what happens when possibilities begin to take form—not just as paths water may follow, but as structures that endure.”

The man looked back at the stream.

“And will that answer my question?”

The teacher began walking toward the village.

“If it does, you will ask a harder one.”

The man followed him, smiling despite himself.

Behind them, the water continued to flow around the stone. The paths it had taken were now part of the stream’s history, while the ground ahead offered new directions.

And the man began to wonder whether becoming was not simply the arrival of what had been possible, but a continuing movement in which what became helped shape what could become next.

Chapter VI — The Bridge Between Two Banks

The following morning, the man returned to the old tree, where the teacher was waiting with his staff in hand.

“I have been thinking about the stream,” the man said.

“I suspected as much. You have the look of a man who has spent the night arguing with a river.”

The man smiled.

“You said that what becomes helps shape what may become next. But I still do not understand how relationship fits into this. We have spoken of the Undivided Potential, of possibility, and of the things that become actual. Where does relationship belong?”

The teacher looked toward the meadow.

“Come. I know a place where we may begin to answer that.”

They followed the familiar path through the grass, passing the scattered trees and the stream they had visited the day before. This time, the teacher continued farther downstream, where the banks narrowed and the water flowed beneath an old wooden bridge.

The bridge was weathered by years of rain and sunlight. Its wooden boards had darkened with age, and its supporting beams rested upon stones at either side of the stream. Though it was not a grand structure, it stood firmly enough for travelers to cross without entering the water. The teacher stepped onto the bridge.

“Tell me,” he said, “what is this for?”

“To cross the stream.”

“And why would someone need it?”

“Because the water separates the two banks. Without the bridge, a traveler would have to find another way across.”

The teacher nodded.

“Then look at the two sides. Are they the same place?”

“No.”

“Does the bridge make them the same?”

“No. It connects them.”

“Precisely.”

The man looked down through the gaps between the boards. The water moved beneath his feet, flowing onward as it had the previous day.

“The bridge does not remove the stream,” he said. “It allows us to cross it.”

“An important distinction. Some people, when faced with a difficulty, imagine that the only solution is to remove one side of it. But sometimes the answer is not to destroy the distance. It is to discover how the distance may be crossed.”

They continued to the middle of the bridge.

The teacher rested his hands upon the wooden railing.

“Imagine that the bridge were taken away. What would happen?”

“We would no longer be able to cross here.”

“And if the two banks remained?”

“They would still be there, but the passage between them would be lost.”

“So the bridge matters because of the relationship it establishes between the two sides. Its importance is not exhausted by the wood from which it is made. It allows something to happen that was not possible in the same way before.”

The man looked from one bank to the other.

“Then relationship is not merely another thing among things. It can change what things are able to do together.”

“Just so.”

The teacher tapped the railing with his staff.

“Consider the boards beneath your feet. Each is distinct from the others. Yet arranged and joined in a particular way, they form a passage. A pile of boards lying in the grass would not serve the same purpose.”

“So their arrangement matters.”

“And the way they are joined matters. A bridge is not simply a collection of wood. It is wood arranged into a structure that connects two sides and bears the weight of those who cross.” The man thought for a moment.

“Then relationship can give rise to something greater than the separate parts considered alone.” “Yes. And that is why we must take care when speaking of relationship. We do not mean merely that two things happen to be near one another. We mean that how things are related can help determine what they become and what becomes possible through them.”

The man looked toward the far bank.

“Then the bridge is a good example of what you mean by relationship.”

“It is a beginning,” the teacher replied. “But do not become too fond of it. If you try to explain the entire universe with a wooden bridge, you may find yourself in need of a larger bridge.” The man laughed.

They stepped off the bridge and walked a short distance along the bank. The teacher stopped beneath a tree whose branches leaned over the water.

“There is another way in which people have spoken about relationship,” he said. “One that reaches beyond the joining of things in the world.”

The man turned toward him.

“What way?”

“Through the person of Jesus, the Christ.”

The man had heard the name before, though he knew little about the man to whom it belonged. “Jesus, the Christ,” he repeated. “Who was he?”

The teacher sat upon a flat stone beside the stream and motioned for the man to sit with him. “Jesus was a man who lived among the people of Judea nearly two thousand years ago. He taught about the kingdom of God, spoke in parables, healed the sick according to the accounts his followers left behind, and called people to love God and their neighbors. He gathered disciples around him, and his life ended in crucifixion under Roman authority.”

The man listened carefully.

“And why do people speak of him as though he were more than a man?”

“Because the people who followed him came to believe that he was more than a man. They spoke of him as the Christ, the Son of God. They told stories of his life, preserved his teachings, and wrote about what they believed had happened through him. They said that he had been crucified and had risen from the dead, and that through him humanity could be reconciled with God.”

The man considered this.

“Then his followers believed that God had come into the world as a person?”

“Yes. And one of them, writing an account of Jesus’ life, expressed the idea in a remarkable way. He wrote that the Word became flesh and dwelt among us.”

The man looked puzzled.

“The Word? Do you mean the words Jesus spoke?”

“Not merely the words he spoke. In the account we call the Gospel according to John, the writer begins by speaking of the Word as being with God and being God. He then tells us that this same Word became flesh. He was not simply describing a teacher who had something

important to say. He was making a much greater claim about who Jesus was.”

The teacher paused, allowing the man to consider this.

“His followers understood Jesus as the living expression of God, the one through whom God made himself known. The Word was not merely a message delivered from a distant source. The Word had entered human life. In Jesus, the unseen God was made known through a human person.”

The man glanced back at the bridge.

“And you say this has something to do with relationship?”

“I do. But we must be careful. The bridge is an illustration, not an explanation of everything these followers were saying about Jesus. They did not simply describe him as a tool God used to cross a distance. They were trying to express something much deeper.”

“Then how should I understand it?”

The teacher picked up a small twig and turned it between his fingers.

“Let us return to what we have been discussing. We began with the Undivided Potential, the Source from which all things could become. We then considered possibility: the ways in which something can take form. Now we are asking how relationship allows distinct things to participate in a shared reality, and how something new may emerge through that relationship.” The man nodded.

“And Jesus?”

“Those who came to understand him as the Son of God also spoke of his relationship with the Father. They did not describe the Father and the Son as two separate gods. Over time, the followers of Jesus developed a way of speaking about this mystery through the teaching of the Trinity: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Three distinct Persons, yet one God.”

The man frowned.

“But how can they be distinct without being separate?”

“That question troubled many people who tried to understand what the followers of Jesus were saying. The Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Holy Spirit. Yet they did not mean that there were three independent gods. They were trying to speak of distinction without separation, of difference that does not destroy unity.”

The man looked toward the two banks again.

“So the bridge might help me picture how distinction does not have to prevent relationship.” “Yes, though the comparison has its limits. The Father and the Son are not two distant places with Jesus serving as a piece of wood between them. The claim made by his followers was that the Son belonged to the very life of God and revealed the Father. Their relationship was not something added afterward. It belonged to who they understood God to be.”

The man was silent for a while.

“Then when they called Jesus the Word, they meant that God made himself known through him?”

“Yes. And they meant even more than that. The writer of John’s Gospel connected the Word to the beginning of all things. He wrote that all things came into being through the Word. Think about the weight of that claim. The one they had known as a man, the one who had walked among them, spoken to them, and shared their world, was now being described as the one through whom the world itself had come to be.”

The man looked down at his hands.

“Why would God do that?”

The teacher’s expression grew more serious.

“Those who followed Jesus spoke of love, reconciliation, and redemption. They believed that God had not abandoned humanity to its estrangement from him. In Jesus, they saw someone who entered into human suffering rather than remaining distant from it. They believed that through his life, death, and resurrection, the way back to communion with God had been opened.”

“Then the bridge is not only about connecting two sides. It is also about someone coming to meet those who cannot cross on their own.”

“That is a thoughtful way to put it,” the teacher said. “The followers of Jesus did not merely say that he pointed toward reconciliation. They said that through him, reconciliation had become possible.”

The man looked at the stream. He remembered the water flowing around the stone, the different paths that had been possible, and the meadow where many trees belonged to one landscape.

He had been thinking of relationship as a connection between things that already existed. Now he was beginning to wonder whether relationship could be more fundamental than

that—whether it could be part of what allowed things to participate in a greater whole at all. “But if the Son is the Word through whom all things were made,” he asked, “then is relationship present from the beginning?”

The teacher smiled gently.

“Yes,” he said. “But perhaps we must reconsider what we mean by the beginning.”

The man tilted his head.

“What do you mean?”

“When we speak of the beginning, we often imagine a time when nothing existed, followed by a moment when something finally did. But what if the Source of all things is not simply the first thing to appear? What if the beginning is not merely a moment in time, but the eternal ground of becoming itself?”

The man considered this.

“You mean the Undivided Potential?”

“Precisely. We have spoken of it as the Source from which all things may become. But perhaps we must understand it not as an empty potential waiting to be filled, but as the eternal potential of becoming itself.”

“And what does that mean?”

“It means that possibility was never merely a collection of things that might happen. Within the Undivided Potential lies the possibility of becoming—the generative movement through which distinction, relationship, and new forms of existence may emerge.”

The man looked toward the stream.

“Then relationship was always there?”

“Yes. Relationship belongs to the eternal generative movement of becoming itself. It is not something that appeared only after distinct things came into existence. It belongs to the very potential through which distinction can emerge, through which possibilities can enter into relation, and through which something new can become.”

The man thought for a moment.

“Then becoming doesn’t begin with separate things coming together. Their ability to enter into relationship is already part of what makes becoming possible.”

The teacher smiled.

“Just so. The things that emerge are new, but the generative potential through which they emerge does not have to be created anew with each thing. The movement of becoming belongs to the deeper reality from which each new form arises.”

The man looked back toward the bridge.

“So the bridge is an example of relationship taking form, but relationship itself reaches further back than the bridge—or even the things that make it.”

“Exactly. The bridge shows us what relationship can accomplish once distinct parts exist. But our inquiry asks a deeper question: what makes the emergence of distinct things, and their capacity to enter into new relationships, possible in the first place?”

The teacher turned toward the path.

“And that brings us back to the Undivided Potential. Unity does not mean a lifeless stillness in which nothing can ever emerge. It is the Source of an eternal generative movement—the potential through which becoming is possible. Relationship belongs to that movement, and through relationship, what becomes may give rise to something more.”

The man looked once more at the bridge, its wooden boards joining two banks that remained distinct.

“When I first came to you,” he said, “I wanted to understand how everything came to be. I wanted to know what lies beneath the things we see.”

The teacher nodded.

“And do you still wish to know?”

“More than ever,” the man replied. “But I think I understand the question differently now. I began by looking for something beneath the world, something that could explain why anything existed at all. Now I wonder whether the answer lies not simply in what reality is, but in how reality becomes.”

The teacher smiled.

“And what have you learned?”

“That the things we see may be only the forms that becoming takes. Beneath them lies the Undivided Potential, the Source from which all things may emerge. Possibility belongs to that Source, and relationship belongs to the eternal generative movement through which possibility becomes something more.”

The teacher regarded him quietly.

“Then you have not yet discovered everything that lies beneath the things you see.” “No,” the man admitted. “But perhaps I have begun to understand what I should be looking for.” The teacher placed a hand upon his shoulder.

“Then let us continue.”

Together they turned toward the village, leaving the old bridge behind them. The two banks remained distinct, the stream continued its journey, and the world around them appeared much as it had before.

Yet the man no longer looked upon it in quite the same way.

He had come searching for the foundation of reality. Now he was beginning to see that the question might reach beyond the things that exist, toward the eternal potential and generative movement through which anything could become at all.

Chapter VII — Beneath the Eternal Stars

The man left the teacher that afternoon with more questions than he had brought with him. They had spoken for much of the morning about the Undivided Potential, about relationship, and about the movement through which one thing might give rise to another. Yet the more he considered their conversation, the more he found himself returning to a question that seemed to stand beneath all the others.

What, precisely, was the Undivided Potential?

The teacher had called it the Source from which all things might become. He had explained that it was not an object among other objects, nor an empty nothingness waiting to be filled. He had even suggested that the generative movement of becoming belonged to its eternal nature. But the man still struggled to understand what any of this meant.

If the Source was undivided, how could distinction emerge from it? If it was potential, what did that potential consist of? And if the movement of becoming was eternal, what did it mean to speak of a beginning at all?

By midday, the teacher had grown hungry, and the man had grown no less curious.

They parted with the understanding that they would continue their discussion another time. But the man found that he could not leave the matter alone.

He went about his afternoon as he usually did. He ate his meal, attended to his work, and exchanged a few words with his neighbors. Yet beneath these ordinary activities, the question remained.

He tried to imagine the Source as a great emptiness stretching beyond the stars, but this only made him wonder what that emptiness was and where it existed.

He imagined it as a light from which all things emerged, but then he wondered whether the light itself was already something distinct.

He even tried to imagine the Source as a vast collection of every possibility, though this seemed to contradict what the teacher had said. A collection, after all, was already composed of distinguishable things.

Every picture he formed seemed to bring him back to the same difficulty.

He was trying to understand what made distinction possible by imagining something distinct. By evening, he could bear it no longer.

He made his way toward the teacher’s house.

The teacher was inside when the man arrived, occupied with the ordinary business of preparing his evening meal. He looked up when he heard the knock at the door.

“You again?” he asked.

“I am afraid so.”

“Have you forgotten something?”

“No.”

“Have you brought me something to eat?”

“No.”

The teacher looked at him with mild disappointment.

“Then I suspect you have brought me another question.”

“I have.”

“Of course you have.”

The teacher set aside what he was doing and invited the man inside.

“What is troubling you now?”

The man sat down, though he seemed unable to settle himself.

“I have been thinking about the Undivided Potential all afternoon. You say it is the Source from which all things may become, but I cannot understand what that means. Every time I try to imagine it, I turn it into something else. If I imagine an emptiness, I have imagined a place. If I imagine a light, I have imagined a thing. If I imagine every possibility gathered together, I have imagined many things instead of one.”

The teacher listened without interrupting.

“And there is something else,” the man continued. “You said that the movement of becoming is eternal. But how can that be? Surely there was a beginning. The stars, the earth, and everything we know must have come into existence somehow. If becoming is eternal, what existed before the world?”

The teacher regarded him for a moment.

Then he rose from his chair.

“Come outside,” he said.

The man followed him through the doorway.

Night had settled over the village. The last traces of daylight had disappeared beyond the distant hills, and the sky above them had opened into a darkness scattered with stars. The teacher stepped into the yard and looked upward.

“Tell me,” he said, “what do you see?”

“The stars.”

“Only the stars?”

“The sky, too.”

“And what lies beyond them?”

The man considered the question.

“I do not know.”

“An honest answer. Let us keep it.”

They stood quietly for a while, looking upward.

The teacher pointed toward a bright cluster of stars above the horizon.

“Those lights are not all the same. Some are nearer to us than others. Some are larger, some smaller. Some may have existed for a very long time before their light reached our eyes. The heavens contain differences of distance, size, age, and form. What we see is a universe filled with distinct things and relationships.”

The man nodded.

“And yet,” the teacher continued, “everything you have pointed to belongs to the physical reality we are trying to understand. The stars are part of that reality. So are the distances between them, the matter from which they formed, and the conditions through which they came to exist.” “Then we must ask what made all of that possible,” the man replied.

“Just so. But be careful. If you search only for the earliest physical thing, you may discover something about the history of the physical universe without yet answering the deeper question you have been asking.”

The man turned toward him.

“What deeper question?”

“What makes becoming possible at all?”

The man looked back at the stars.

The teacher continued.

“When you first came to me, you wanted to know how a seed became a tree. You learned that the seed alone was not enough. The soil, the water, the light, and the conditions surrounding it all mattered. Then you asked where those conditions came from. We followed that question until you began to wonder about the Source of reality itself.”

“I remember.”

“Now you are asking about the stars, but the structure of your question has not changed. You want to know how physical things came to be. Yet the Source you are trying to understand cannot simply be another physical thing, for then we would have to ask how that thing became possible.”

The man was silent.

“Then the Undivided Potential must be something other than physical reality.”

“That is the distinction we must make,” said the teacher. “We are considering the possibility that the ground of becoming is not itself merely one more form within the physical universe. If it were, it would belong to the very order of things whose emergence we are trying to understand.”

The man looked upward again.

“But what does that tell us about the Source itself?”

“Only that we must be careful about how we imagine it. We cannot simply take something familiar from the world and make it infinitely large, infinitely old, or infinitely powerful. The question is not how to imagine a greater object. It is how to understand what makes the emergence of objects and their relationships possible in the first place.”

The man thought for a while.

“Then perhaps the Undivided Potential is like a vast emptiness from which everything emerged.” The teacher shook his head gently.

“Would an emptiness not already be something you have imagined? A place without objects is still a place. And if you imagine the Source as a place, you have already assumed space. You have given it a condition that belongs to the world you are trying to understand.” The man frowned.

“Then I cannot even call it empty?”

“You may use the word if you understand its limitations. But do not mistake the absence of familiar things for the absence of all distinction. We are asking about a ground that cannot be adequately described by treating it as one more condition within an already existing world.” The man folded his arms.

“You make it difficult to think about.”

“It is difficult to think about. That is why we are thinking about it.”

The teacher smiled, and the man could not help smiling in return.

After a moment, the man asked, “Then what do you mean when you say that becoming is eternal?”

The teacher looked up at the stars again.

“Consider what we have learned. A thing becomes what it is through conditions and relationships. What becomes may then enter into new relationships, opening possibilities that were not actual before. Becoming is not just the appearance of a finished thing. It is the generative movement through which new forms can emerge.”

The man nodded.

“And you believe that movement has always existed?”

“I am saying that, within the framework we are developing, we must not confuse the beginning of physical actuality with the beginning of becoming itself. If becoming is the eternal generative movement through which actuality is possible, then its origin cannot simply be identified with the first appearance of a physical object.”

The man looked toward the distant hills, where the last warmth of the day had disappeared. “Then before the stars, before the earth, before anything physical existed, becoming was already possible?”

“More than merely possible,” the teacher replied. “We are considering becoming as belonging eternally to the nature of the Source. The emergence of physical reality would then be an expression of that generative movement, not the event that first brought the movement into existence.”

The man was quiet for a long time.

He had been imagining eternity as though it meant an endless stretch of time, reaching backward without ever finding a first moment. But now he began to see that the teacher was making a different kind of claim.

If time itself belonged to the order of physical becoming, then perhaps the eternal Source could not be understood simply as something that had been waiting for an extraordinarily long time before the universe appeared.

“Then when I ask what happened before the universe,” the man said slowly, “I may already be assuming that time existed before the thing whose emergence I am trying to understand.” The teacher nodded.

“That is the difficulty. We must distinguish between asking about an earlier event within time and asking about the ground from which physical reality, including its temporal order, may emerge. They are not necessarily the same question.”

The man looked upward.

The stars seemed unchanged, scattered across the darkness as they had been a moment before. Yet he found himself looking at them differently.

He had come searching for something that existed before the stars, imagining that he might discover an earlier object, a more ancient substance, or a hidden place beyond the visible heavens.

Now he was beginning to understand why the teacher had resisted those images.

The Source was not being proposed as another object hidden somewhere behind the universe. It was being considered as the ground of the generative movement through which anything could take form.

“And the Undivided Potential,” the man said, “is the name you give to that Source?” “Yes. It names the foundational potential from which distinction, relationship, and becoming may arise. But remember that a name is not an explanation. We must still examine what we mean by it and how the movement we are describing can be understood.”

The man turned toward him.

“Then we have not yet explained how the Undivided gives rise to distinction.”

“No,” the teacher replied. “We have only reached the point where we can ask the question more carefully.”

The man sighed.

“I had hoped that coming here tonight would settle something.”

“And has it?”

The man considered the stars once more.

“I think I understand why I cannot picture the Source as a thing. And I understand why the beginning of physical reality need not be the beginning of becoming itself. But I still do not understand how the first distinction becomes possible.”

The teacher placed his hands behind his back.

“Then you have made progress.”

“It does not feel like much.”

“Perhaps not. But a man who has learned to recognize the assumptions hidden inside his questions is not standing where he stood before.”

They remained beneath the stars for a while longer.

The man thought about the seed, the meadow, the stream, and the bridge. Each had shown him something about the way things became and the relationships through which they took form. Yet none had been the Source itself. Each belonged to the world of things already distinguished. He began to suspect that the next step would require more than finding another object to examine.

It would require understanding the movement through which distinction and relationship could become possible at all.

At last, he turned toward the teacher.

“If the generative movement of becoming is eternal,” he asked, “then what is the first movement through which the Undivided becomes differentiated?”

The teacher looked toward the horizon, where the stars continued to shine.

“Now,” he said, “we are approaching the question that must come next.”

And beneath the ancient heavens, the man understood that he had not yet reached the beginning of his inquiry.

He had only begun to discover what a beginning might mean.

Chapter VIII — The Desire to Give

The following morning, the man returned to the teacher’s house.

He had slept little the night before. The conversation beneath the stars had followed him home, and the teacher’s final words had remained with him long after he had laid down to rest. Perhaps the first movement was not a breaking apart.

Perhaps it was a giving.

The thought had seemed simple when he first heard it. Yet the more he considered it, the more questions it produced.

If the Source was undivided, what could it give? If nothing distinct yet existed, who or what could receive its gift? And if the desire to give belonged to the very nature of the Source, what did that tell him about the nature of reality?

He found the teacher outside, sitting beneath the old tree with a cup in his hand. “You look tired,” the teacher observed.

“I have been thinking.”

“I had gathered as much. You have the appearance of a man who has been defeated by a thought and intends to demand a rematch.”

The man sat beside him.

“Yesterday, you suggested that the first movement of becoming might be understood as a giving. I have been trying to understand what you meant.”

The teacher took another sip from his cup.

“And have you succeeded?”

“No.”

“Good. I was beginning to worry that you might have learned something.”

The man smiled, but his thoughts quickly returned to the question.

“You said that the Source might give because giving belongs to its nature. But I do not understand how that can be. When a man gives something, he gives it to someone. There must be a giver and a receiver. There must be something that passes between them. But if the Source is undivided, where are these distinctions?”

The teacher set his cup upon the ground.

“That is a good place to begin. Tell me, why does a man give something to another?” “Because the other person needs it, perhaps. Or because he wishes to help him.”

“And if he gives because he has been forced to do so?”

“Then he gives unwillingly.”

“And if he gives only because he expects something in return?”

“Then he may be giving for his own benefit.”

The teacher nodded.

“So we must distinguish between giving that arises from necessity, giving that arises from obligation, and giving that arises from a desire to give.”

The man considered this.

“But even a man who gives freely must have something to give.”

“Usually,” the teacher replied. “Though I have known men who give advice when they possess very little wisdom. They seem to have an inexhaustible supply.”

The man laughed.

“But your question is important. In our ordinary experience, giving involves things that already exist. A man gives bread, a neighbor receives it, and their relationship changes through the

exchange. We cannot simply take that familiar situation and imagine it occurring before all things exist.”

“Then how can we speak of giving at the beginning of reality?”

“Perhaps we must ask whether we are speaking of an event that occurred at some moment in time, or of something more fundamental: the nature of the Source from which becoming is possible.”

The man leaned forward.

“You mean that giving might not be something the Source decided to do after existing for a time?”

“Precisely. If we are considering the Source as eternal, we should be careful not to imagine it sitting in an empty place, waiting through an endless stretch of time before deciding to create something. That would already place the Source within a sequence of events.”

The man looked down at the ground.

“Then the desire to give is not necessarily a decision that occurred before the universe.” “Not as we are presently considering it. We are asking whether giving expresses the eternal nature of the Source, rather than describing an event that happened to the Source at some earlier moment.”

The man sat quietly for a while.

“But there is something I still do not understand.”

The teacher waited.

“If nothing existed before the universe, then who was the Source giving to? Giving requires someone to receive. If there was nothing distinct from the Source, then how could there be giving at all?”

The teacher regarded him thoughtfully.

“Perhaps we have reached a point where we must reconsider what we mean when we speak of God as one.”

The man looked up.

“What do you mean?”

“Jesus teaches that God is understood as one God in three Persons: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. They are not three separate gods, nor are they three parts that must be

assembled to make God complete. They are distinct Persons who share the one divine essence.”

The man considered this.

“Then the Father was never alone?”

“Not in the sense of existing without relationship. The Son and the Holy Spirit are not beings who came into existence after the Father, nor did God first exist without relationship and later acquire it through creation. In the followers of Christ’s understanding, the three Persons belong eternally to the one divine life.”

The man looked toward the distant hills.

“Then relationship did not have to wait for the universe to exist.”

“Precisely. If we are to understand giving as belonging eternally to the nature of God, we must also take seriously Christ’s understanding that relationship belongs eternally to God. The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are not a solution invented after creation to explain how God could relate to something outside himself. Their relationship belongs to the divine life itself.” The man was quiet for a moment.

“Then the Source did not have to create something in order to experience relationship.” “That is the significance of the Trinity for our inquiry. Creation does not supply God with a relationship that God previously lacked. In Christ’s understanding, God is eternally relational. What creation brings about is not the beginning of relationship within God, but the emergence of a world distinct from God and capable of entering into relationship with him.” The man slowly nodded.

“But if relationship already belongs to God’s eternal nature, then why does God give rise to anything beyond himself?”

The teacher smiled.

“Perhaps we should begin by considering the difference between giving because something is missing and giving because giving expresses what one is.”

He rose from beneath the tree and motioned for the man to follow him.

“Come. Let us walk.”

They followed a narrow path toward the meadow. The morning sun had begun to warm the grass, and a gentle breeze moved through the branches overhead.

“For example,” the teacher continued, “if God required the world in order to become complete, creation would supply something God lacked. But if God is eternally complete in the communion of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, then we must consider another possibility: that creation is not the remedy for a deficiency, but the expression of a goodness that does not need to be completed.”

The man considered this.

“Then the world was not created because God was lonely.”

“That is not how Christ’s understanding of God would have us begin. The Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are not an isolated being waiting for company. We are asking whether the creation of the world can be understood as a free expression of divine goodness rather than an act driven by need.”

The man looked toward the teacher.

“And you believe that this giving reveals something about the nature of the Source itself?” “I do. But we must follow the question carefully. We have considered why giving need not arise from lack. Now we must ask what giving reveals about the one who gives—and how that understanding might help us think about the first differentiation.”

As they continued through the meadow, the teacher stopped beside a flowering tree. “Tell me what you see.”

“A tree.”

“I had hoped we might progress beyond that, but go on.”

The man smiled.

“It has flowers.”

“And what will become of them?”

“Some may develop into fruit. Others may fall away.”

“And what do the flowers give?”

The man examined the branches.

“They may produce fruit that provides food. Their pollen may help other plants reproduce.” The teacher nodded.

“Consider what is happening here. The tree does not just exist as an isolated object. It participates in a wider movement of life. Its flowers contribute to what may come after them. Its fruit can nourish other creatures, and its seeds may give rise to new trees.”

“So the tree gives something to the world around it.”

“Yes. But do not misunderstand the example. The tree does not possess human intentions, and its biological processes do not prove anything about the origin of the universe. We are using it to consider a pattern: what exists may participate in the emergence of something beyond its present form.”

The man looked at the branches.

“And you believe this pattern reaches all the way back to the Source?”

“I believe that is the question we must investigate. If relationship belongs to the generative movement of becoming, then perhaps giving is not merely something that happens after things become distinct. Perhaps the possibility of giving expresses something more fundamental about the Source itself.”

The man frowned thoughtfully.

“But if the Source is undivided, it cannot give in the same way that the tree gives its fruit.” “Correct. We must not confuse the illustration with the thing we are trying to understand. The tree helps us think about giving, but the Source is not a tree, and its generative movement is not a biological process.”

“Then what does the Source give?”

The teacher looked toward the meadow.

“Perhaps the first thing we should understand is that giving need not mean transferring an object from one place to another. We are considering whether the Source gives rise to distinction itself: whether what becomes distinct can emerge from the Undivided without requiring the destruction of the unity from which it emerges.”

The man slowly nodded.

“So the first giving would not be the transfer of something already existing. It would be the emergence of what can exist.”

“That is the direction of our inquiry.”

The man looked at the trees scattered across the meadow.

The teacher stopped walking.

“Be careful with the word reason. We have not yet demonstrated that the origin of reality must be understood in precisely this way. We are developing a philosophical interpretation. Within that interpretation, the desire to give offers a way to understand differentiation not as an arbitrary fracture, but as an expression of the Source’s nature.”

The man considered this.

“And what is that nature?”

The teacher turned toward him.

“That is where we must speak about relationship.”

They continued along the path until they reached the stream.

The water moved around the stones, dividing into small currents before coming together again downstream. The man watched it for a moment, remembering their earlier conversations. “I used to think of relationship as something that happened between things,” he said. “Two things existed, and then they became related.”

“That is how we ordinarily encounter relationships,” the teacher replied. “We meet another person, build a friendship, or join our efforts with someone else’s. The relationship seems to arise between people who already exist.”

“And yet you have been suggesting that relationship is more fundamental than that.” “Yes. Our inquiry asks whether relationship is just an arrangement among things that already exist, or whether it belongs to the generative ground through which things become distinct and capable of entering into relationships at all.”

The man watched the currents rejoin.

“If relationship belongs to the Source, then it cannot have begun only after separate things existed.”

“That is the possibility we are exploring. Within this framework, relationship is not merely an addition to existence. It belongs to the eternal movement through which becoming is possible.” The man looked toward the teacher.

“Then the Source is relationship?”

The teacher paused before answering.

“I would put it this way: we are considering whether the Source is relational in its very nature. Relationship would not be something foreign to it, something it acquired after creation. It would belong to what the Source eternally is.”

“But how can relationship exist without distinct things?”

The teacher looked at the water.

“Perhaps we must distinguish between relationship as we encounter it among already-existing things and relationship as the generative principle through which distinction itself becomes possible. The first is familiar to us. The second is what we are trying to understand.” The man remained silent.

He had spent much of his life imagining reality as a collection of separate objects. Even when he thought about the whole universe, he pictured a great collection of things, spread across an immense expanse of space.

Now he was being asked to consider whether the relationships through which things became what they were might be more fundamental than the things themselves.

“If relationship belongs to the Source,” he said at last, “then perhaps the Source does not give because it first encounters something outside itself that it wishes to help. Perhaps giving expresses the nature of the Source, and the emergence of distinction is how that giving becomes manifest.”

The teacher smiled.

“You are beginning to understand what I mean.”

The man looked down at the stream again.

“But we have been speaking in abstractions. We have talked about the Source, relationship, and giving. How can we know whether this is more than an idea?”

The teacher’s expression grew thoughtful.

“In Jesus’ teachings, we are given a way to consider that question through the person of Jesus Christ.”

The man looked up.

“Jesus again?”

“Yes. We spoke about him when we stood upon the bridge. We considered the claim, in the followers of Christ’s understanding, that the Word through whom all things came into being became flesh and lived among us. But perhaps we did not yet ask what that means for our understanding of the Source.”

The teacher sat upon a flat stone beside the stream, and the man sat with him.

“Those who followed Jesus did not just remember him as a teacher who spoke about God,” the teacher continued. “They came to believe that in him, God had made himself known. They saw his life as a revelation of the Father’s nature.”

“What did they see?”

“They saw someone who welcomed the rejected, served others, showed compassion toward the suffering, and taught his followers to love even their enemies. He spoke of loving God and one’s neighbor. He washed the feet of his disciples. He called people away from self-exaltation and toward service.”

The man listened carefully.

“But many teachers have spoken about love. What made Jesus different?”

“He did not just teach love. He gave himself.”

The teacher paused, looking toward the water.

“His life reached its culmination in his self-giving on the cross. He accepted death rather than abandon the mission he had undertaken. In the followers of Christ’s understanding, that death was not an isolated act of generosity, but part of God’s work of reconciliation and redemption. They believe that in Jesus, God entered human suffering and gave himself for humanity.”

The man was quiet.

“So the cross reveals the nature of the Source?”

“That is what I believe. If we want to understand Jesus’ teaching that God is love, we must look at the life of Jesus as a whole, and especially at his self-giving on the cross. There, love is not merely described. It is enacted. The one who taught his followers to love gave his own life for them.”

The man looked at the teacher.

“Then the desire to give is not simply a principle we have imagined to explain the beginning of the universe. You see it revealed in Jesus.”

“Yes. The metaphysical argument and Christ’s revelation are distinct, and we must not pretend that one automatically proves the other. But within my understanding, they illuminate one another. The philosophical inquiry asks whether giving expresses the generative nature of the Source. In the followers of Christ’s understanding, Jesus is the revelation of that nature.” The man lowered his eyes.

“And what do you believe Jesus reveals about the Source?”

The teacher answered without hesitation.

“That the Source is not defined by isolation or indifference. That love is not just a response to something outside itself. That giving can express the very nature of the giver. And that the deepest form of this giving is not simply handing over something one possesses, but offering oneself for the good of another.”

The man looked toward the stream.

“But if the Source is undivided, who is the other for whom it gives?”

The teacher nodded slowly.

“That is an important question. We must be careful not to imagine the Source as though it were a lonely person waiting for someone to love. In Christ’s understanding, the relationship of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit belongs eternally to the life of God. God does not first become relational by creating the world.”

The man turned toward him.

“Then relationship already belongs to the Source, even before physical reality exists?” “Yes. The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are distinct Persons, yet one God. Their distinction does not divide the divine unity into three independent gods. In Christ’s understanding, their relationship is eternal.”

The man considered this.

“And Jesus, the Son, is the one who became human?”

“Yes. The eternal Word became flesh in Jesus Christ. He was not merely a messenger sent to tell humanity about a distant God. In the followers of Christ’s understanding, in him God made himself known.” The man looked back toward the stream.

“Then the Source does not need to create something in order to have relationship.” “Precisely. And if the Source is not incomplete, then perhaps we should not understand creation as something God needs in order to become what he is.”

The man was quiet for a moment.

“Then giving would not be an attempt to fill something that is missing.”

“That is the possibility we are exploring. Perhaps giving is not a remedy for a deficiency, but an expression of the nature of the one who gives.”

The man looked toward the water, watching it divide around a stone before joining together again.

“Then the many do not have to be enemies of the One.”

“No,” the teacher replied. “Difference need not mean separation. We are asking whether distinction can emerge without destroying the unity from which it comes.”

The man smiled faintly.

“I think I understand what you are proposing. But there is still something I do not understand.” The teacher sighed.

“I had almost allowed myself to hope.”

The man laughed.

“If giving expresses the nature of the Source, and relationship belongs to that nature, then why does giving take the form of physical reality? Why stars, planets, living creatures, and human beings? Why does the generative movement of becoming produce this world rather than something else?”

The teacher looked toward the meadow.

For a long moment, he said nothing.

“Can you not answer?” the man asked.

The teacher turned toward him.

“Not completely. We can consider what the world reveals to us. We can investigate its structures, trace its history, and ask how one form gives rise to another. We can even develop a philosophical understanding of why the emergence of distinction might belong to the nature of the Source. But why reality takes precisely this form, rather than another, is a question I cannot claim to have answered.”

The man looked disappointed.

“Then perhaps we have reached the end of our inquiry.”

“Perhaps we have reached the end of what I can tell you with certainty,” the teacher replied. “That is not quite the same thing.”

The man considered this.

“Then what can we say?”

The teacher looked toward the distant hills.

“If Christ’s understanding is true, then the Source is not an isolated being who creates because it is lonely or incomplete. The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit belong eternally to the divine life. Relationship does not begin with the universe.”

He paused.

“But creation allows something further: beings distinct from God who can come to know him, respond to him, and enter into relationship with him. And among those beings, there are creatures who can become conscious of their own existence, wonder about their Source, and freely choose how they will respond.”

The man looked down at the ground.

“Then God created conscious beings so that they might know him?”

“That is how I understand it. Not because God lacked someone to love, but because relationship belongs to his nature. The creation of conscious beings makes possible a relationship in which love can be recognized, received, and freely returned.”

The man looked toward the sky.

“Then our existence is not simply a consequence of the universe becoming complicated enough to produce us. Within your understanding, we are also beings capable of entering into a relationship with the Source from which we came.”

“Yes. Though we must be careful not to confuse our interpretation of purpose with a scientific demonstration of why the universe exists. We can investigate how conscious life emerged, but the question of why there is a world in which consciousness and relationship become possible reaches beyond what I can establish.”

The man was quiet for a while.

“And you are content to leave that question unanswered?”

The teacher smiled.

“Content? Not entirely. But I would rather admit where my understanding ends than pretend that the mystery has disappeared simply because I have found words for it.”

The man laughed softly.

“I suppose that is why you have spent so much time answering my questions with more questions.”

“Someone must keep you occupied.”

They both smiled.

The man looked toward the stream, where the water continued its quiet movement around the stones.

He had begun his inquiry by asking why anything existed at all. He did not know why the universe had taken the form it had. He did not know how every stage of becoming had unfolded, or why consciousness had emerged in the particular forms it had.

But he had come to see that these unanswered questions did not make the inquiry

meaningless.

Perhaps the Source did not give because something was missing.

Perhaps it gave because giving expressed what it was.

And perhaps, in the emergence of conscious beings capable of knowing, loving, and seeking their Creator, that giving had opened the possibility of a relationship unlike any other. The man turned toward the teacher.

“Then perhaps the question is not only why anything exists, but what it means that we exist as beings capable of asking why.”

The teacher smiled.

“That is a question worth carrying with you.”

The man looked once more toward the distant horizon.

“And perhaps one day we will understand more.”

“Perhaps,” the teacher replied. “But understanding more does not require us to pretend that we understand everything.”

The two remained beneath the old tree as the afternoon light spread across the meadow. The man had not discovered a final answer to the mystery of existence. He had discovered something that, in its own way, was more valuable: a way of asking the question that allowed him to see the world, and his own place within it, differently.

And as the stream continued toward places neither man could see, the man began to wonder whether the deepest mystery was not simply that something had become, but that what had become could awaken, look back toward its Source, and ask why it had been given life at all.
